Profit is an opinion, cash is a fact. The three statements are one system: net income links them, and every change must keep the balance sheet balanced.

Read it as a loop: net income is the last line of the income statement, the first line of the cash flow statement, and (minus dividends) the change in retained earnings. The bottom of the cash flow statement is the cash line on the balance sheet.

Standardantwort auf Deutsch: „Führen Sie mich durch die drei Abschlüsse“

About 60 seconds spoken. Say what each statement shows, then the links in the order of the cash flow statement, then close the loop.

1 · Was jeder Abschluss zeigt

  • Die GuV zeigt Erträge und Aufwendungen einer Periode, vom Umsatz bis zum Jahresüberschuss.
  • Die Bilanz zeigt an einem Stichtag das Vermögen (Aktiva) und dessen Finanzierung durch Eigen- und Fremdkapital (Passiva). Beide Seiten sind immer gleich groß.
  • Die Kapitalflussrechnung zeigt die tatsächlichen Zahlungsströme: operativ, Investition, Finanzierung.

2 · Wie sie zusammenhängen

  • Jahresüberschuss: letzte Zeile der GuV, erste Zeile der Kapitalflussrechnung; erhöht abzüglich Dividenden die Gewinnrücklagen.
  • Abschreibungen: mindern das Ergebnis, werden im operativen Cashflow wieder addiert, senken das Anlagevermögen.
  • Working Capital: Forderungen, Vorräte, Verbindlichkeiten aus L+L stehen in der Bilanz; ihre Veränderung läuft durch den operativen Cashflow. Ein Aufbau bindet Cash.
  • CapEx: nicht in der GuV, sondern im Investitions-Cashflow; erhöht das Anlagevermögen.
  • Finanzierung: Kredite, Tilgung, Kapitalerhöhung, Dividenden stehen im Finanzierungs-Cashflow und verändern Finanzschulden bzw. Eigenkapital.

3 · Der Kreis schließt sich

Die Summe der drei Cashflows ist die Veränderung der liquiden Mittel. Anfangsbestand plus Veränderung ergibt den Endbestand, und der ist die Position Kasse in der Bilanz.

Merkhilfe: Jahresüberschuss → Abschreibungen → Working Capital → CapEx → Finanzierung → Kasse. Das ist die Kapitalflussrechnung von oben nach unten.

Trap. A profitable company can go bankrupt. Growth ties up cash in receivables and inventory, CapEx never touches the income statement, and debt repayments are not expenses. Always ask where the cash is.

German accounting words that change the answer

Three kinds of effect

  • erfolgswirksam hits the income statement
  • bilanzwirksam moves the balance sheet
  • zahlungswirksam moves cash

Depreciation is the first two but not the third. Buying a machine for cash is the last two but not the first.

Liabilities are not all debt

  • Verbindlichkeiten aus L+L trade payables: working capital
  • Rückstellungen provisions: operating ones are working capital
  • Pensionsrückstellungen pension provisions: debt-like, go in the bridge M2
  • Finanzverbindlichkeiten financial debt

Under German HGB, goodwill is amortised (usually over 10 years if no life can be estimated) and prudence dominates (Vorsichtsprinzip, Niederstwertprinzip). Under IFRS and US GAAP, goodwill is only impairment-tested. Listed German groups report under IFRS; family companies often report under HGB, so ask which one you are reading.

M2

Enterprise value and the equity bridge

Enterprise value is the price of the business itself, whoever finances it. Equity value is what is left for shareholders after everyone with a prior claim is paid.

EV = Equity value + financial debt + pension deficit + lease liabilities + minorities + preferred − cash − non-operating financial assets Gesamtunternehmenswert = Eigenkapitalwert + Nettofinanzverbindlichkeiten (+ schuldähnliche Posten)

Why each item is there

  • Debt, pensions, leases: a buyer inherits or refinances them.
  • Minorities Minderheitenanteile: EBITDA includes 100% of a subsidiary you own only partly, so the claim of the other owners must be added.
  • Cash: non-operating, and the buyer gets it, so it reduces the net price.
  • Test: does it bear interest or sit in financing? Then it is debt. Does it move with daily business? Then it is working capital.

Matching rule for multiples

  • Metric before interest (revenue, EBITDA, EBIT) pairs with EV.
  • Metric after interest (net income, EPS) pairs with equity value.
  • EV/net income mixes the two and is wrong.
  • EV does not change when a company swaps cash for debt repayment or raises debt and holds the cash. Equity value also stays put; only the mix moves.

Diluted shares: treasury stock method

Example: 100 shares at $10, 10 options with a $10 strike. The options are at the money, so exercise proceeds of $100 buy back all 10 new shares. Net dilution zero, diluted equity value $1,000.

Change the strike to $5: proceeds $50 buy back 5 shares, net 5 new shares, diluted equity value 105 × $10 = $1,050.

Only in-the-money options count. Convertibles: if in the money, treat as shares and remove from debt; if not, leave as debt. Never do both.

Trap. “A company with EV of 1,000 takes on 500 of debt. What is EV?” Still 1,000 if the cash stays on the balance sheet: debt +500, cash +500. EV changes only when the operating business changes.

M3

Multiples: trading comps and precedent transactions

A multiple is a compressed DCF. It says what the market pays today for one euro of earnings in a business with similar growth, risk and returns.

Trading comps · Börsenmultiplikatoren

  1. Pick 5 to 10 listed peers: same sector, size, growth, margin, geography.
  2. Build EV for each with the bridge M2.
  3. Clean the metric: remove one-offs, align year-ends, use next-twelve-months figures.
  4. Take the median, not the mean; drop negative multiples.
  5. Apply to the target. Result is a minority, liquid value with no premium.

Precedents · Transaktionsmultiplikatoren

  1. Find deals for similar targets in the last 3 to 5 years.
  2. Use the price paid, so the multiple includes a control premium and part of the synergies.
  3. Usually based on last-twelve-months numbers.
  4. Check the context: boom or crisis, auction or bilateral, cash or stock.
  5. Few data points and stale markets are the weakness.

DCF and cost of capital

A company is worth the cash it will hand to its investors, discounted at the return those investors demand. Everything else is detail.

Unlevered FCF = EBIT × (1 − t) + D&A − CapEx − increase in NWC Muster AG: = 100 × 0.75 + 20 − 30 − 10 = 55 Cost of equity (CAPM) = risk-free + beta × market risk premium = 2.5% + 1.15 × 5.5% = 8.8% WACC = E/V × cost of equity + D/V × cost of debt × (1 − t) = 84% × 8.8% + 16% × 5% × 0.75 ≈ 8.0% Terminal value (Gordon growth) = FCF next year ÷ (WACC − g) Simple check: 55 × 1.02 ÷ (8% − 2%) = EV 935 → − net debt 67.5 = equity 867.5 → ÷ 45m = €19.28

The market says €16, the DCF says €19.28, the offer is €20. That gap is the conversation in M6: the buyer pays above standalone value and needs synergies to justify it.

WACC inputs you must be able to defend

  • Risk-free Basiszins: long government bond in the cash-flow currency.
  • Beta: unlever peer betas, take the median, relever at the target's structure. βL = βU × (1 + (1 − t) × D/E).
  • Market risk premium Marktrisikoprämie: roughly 5 to 7%.
  • Weights: market values and target structure, not book.
  • Debt is cheaper than equity because it ranks first and interest is tax-deductible. Past some point, distress costs push WACC back up.

Terminal value sanity checks

  • Often 60 to 80% of EV. Say so before the interviewer does.
  • g must stay below long-run nominal GDP growth, about 1 to 2.5%.
  • Growth costs money: reinvestment rate = g ÷ ROIC. If ROIC equals WACC, growth adds no value.
  • Cross-check: back out the exit multiple implied by your g, and the g implied by your multiple.
  • Mid-year convention raises value slightly because cash arrives through the year.

Landkarte auf Deutsch: WACC von unten nach oben

Wo fängt man an, was ist Asset Beta, und wo kommt die Verschuldung ins Spiel? In der Reihenfolge, in der du rechnest. Zahlen aus einem Beispiel.

WACC 8,37 % ├─ Eigenkapital, 60 % Gewicht × Cost of Equity 11,6 % │ Cost of Equity = Risk-Free + Beta × MRP = 3,0 % + 1,56 × 5,5 % │ Beta × MRP = Company Risk Premium = 8,6 % └─ Fremdkapital, 40 % Gewicht × Cost of Debt nach Steuern 3,5 % Cost of Debt = Risk-Free + Debt Premium = 3,0 % + 1,9 % = 4,9 % nach Steuern: × (1 − 28 %) = 3,5 % WACC = E/(D+E) × CoE + D/(D+E) × CoD × (1 − t)

Risk-Free Rate

Rendite einer sicheren Staatsanleihe (10 oder 20 Jahre). Startpunkt für Eigen- und Fremdkapital.

Market Risk Premium (MRP)

Zusatzrendite für das Risiko des ganzen Aktienmarkts. Meist 5 bis 6 %.

Beta

Wie stark die Aktie auf Marktschwankungen reagiert. 1,0 = wie der Markt, 1,56 = 56 % stärker.

Company Risk Premium

Beta × MRP. Der Aufschlag für das Risiko genau dieser Firma.

Debt Premium

Aufschlag der Gläubiger auf die Risk-Free Rate für das Ausfallrisiko.

Tax Shield

Zinsen mindern die Steuer, deshalb zählt die Cost of Debt nur nach Steuern: × (1 − t).

Probier: Fremdkapital-Anteil hoch → Levered Beta und Cost of Equity steigen, der WACC sinkt trotzdem, weil billige Schulden mehr Gewicht bekommen. Dann den Debt Premium hochdrehen und schauen, wann der Vorteil kippt.

Warum ist Fremdkapital billiger als Eigenkapital?

Der Gläubiger trägt weniger Risiko: Er wird vor den Eigentümern bedient, hat feste Zinsen und Sicherheiten. Und Zinsen sind steuerlich absetzbar, die Cost of Debt wird mit (1 − t) multipliziert.

Warum nicht einfach nur Schulden nehmen?

Mehr Schulden erhöhen das Risiko für Eigentümer (Beta und Cost of Equity steigen) und für Gläubiger (Debt Premium steigt) und bringen Insolvenzrisiko. Ab einem Punkt überwiegen diese Kosten den Tax Shield.

Was ändert sich, wenn die Zielfirma mehr Schulden aufnimmt?

Das Asset Beta bleibt gleich, weil sich das Geschäft nicht ändert. Levered Beta und Cost of Equity steigen. Das Gewicht der billigeren Schulden im WACC steigt ebenfalls.

Ist Cost of Debt oder Cost of Equity höher?

Cost of Equity, fast immer. Im Beispiel: 11,6 % gegen 4,9 % vor bzw. 3,5 % nach Steuern.

Flow to equity: the recipe

  1. Start from net income (after interest and actual taxes).
  2. + D&A − CapEx − increase in working capital.
  3. + new borrowing − debt repayments. This line is what people forget.
  4. Discount at the cost of equity, never at WACC.
  5. Result is equity value. Do not subtract net debt again.

Use for banks and insurers, where debt is part of operations, and when a fixed debt schedule is known. The dividend discount model is the same idea using dividends.

APV: the recipe

  1. Unlevered free cash flow, the same as in the WACC method.
  2. Discount at the unlevered cost of equity → value without debt.
  3. For each year: interest × tax rate = tax shield. Discount it (at the cost of debt if the debt plan is fixed) and add.
  4. Optionally subtract expected distress costs.
  5. Then the normal bridge to equity.

Use when leverage changes every year, as in an LBO: WACC would need a new rate each year, APV keeps one rate and shows the financing benefit on its own line.

The classic mistake: mixing cash flow and rate. Unlevered cash flow goes with WACC (or the unlevered rate in APV); cash flow to equity goes with the cost of equity. And the tax shield may be counted once only: in the WACC, or as an APV block, or in net income.

Trap. “WACC goes up; what happens?” Value falls. “Debt goes up; what happens to WACC?” First down (cheap, tax-shielded debt replaces equity), then up (default risk raises both costs). And a DCF does not work for banks or pre-revenue companies: use dividend discount or real options instead.

Case tip. A typical 90-minute Excel case asks you to build exactly this structure from condensed financials. State your assumptions aloud as you make them; that is what is graded.

M5

Why deals happen, and who wins

A deal creates value only if the two companies are worth more together than apart: V(A+B) > V(A) + V(B). The premium then decides how that gain is split.

Good reasons

  • Synergies: cost (overlap, purchasing, plants) and revenue (cross-selling). Cost synergies are believed, revenue synergies are discounted.
  • Better management: the market for corporate control replaces weak teams.
  • Relief of financial constraints: a cash-rich buyer funds a constrained target.
  • Technology, talent, market entry: faster than building.
  • Tax: losses, step-ups.

Doubtful reasons

  • Diversification: shareholders can diversify themselves for free.
  • EPS accretion alone: arithmetic, not value M6.
  • Empire building and hubris: managers gain from size.
  • Market power: good for shareholders, bad for customers, and regulators block it M9.
  • Fashion: buying because rivals are buying.

Types: horizontal (competitor), vertical (supplier or customer), conglomerate (unrelated). Waves start with change: deregulation, new technology such as AI, cheap financing, shocks, high valuations.

Landkarte auf Deutsch: Wozu ein Merger Model?

Es beantwortet eine Frage: Steigt oder sinkt der Gewinn pro Aktie (EPS) des Käufers durch die Übernahme? Steigt er, ist der Deal accretive; sinkt er, dilutive.

1 · Beide Firmen projizieren

Income Statements von Käufer und Target, oft 3 bis 5 Jahre.

2 · Kaufpreis und Finanzierung

Meist Aktienkurs des Targets plus Prämie Übernahmeprämie. Dazu der Mix: wie viel Cash, Schulden, neue Aktien.

3 · Synergien annehmen

Kosteneinsparungen oder Mehrumsätze durch den Zusammenschluss M5.

4 · Income Statements kombinieren

Gewinne addieren, dann die Finanzierung einrechnen:

  • Cash: entgangene Zinsen auf das ausgegebene Cash
  • Schulden: zusätzliche Zinsen
  • Aktien: zusätzliche Aktien im Nenner
  • Write-ups: Abschreibung auf aufgewertete Assets

5 · Pro-forma-EPS

Kombinierter Nettogewinn ÷ kombinierte Aktienzahl.

6 · Vergleichen

Pro-forma-EPS gegen EPS des Käufers ohne Deal. Höher = accretive, niedriger = dilutive.

Nebenher entstehen in der Bilanz Goodwill und immaterielle Werte (siehe Kaufpreisallokation unten). Für „accretive oder dilutive“ reicht aber meist das Income Statement.

Ich projiziere beide Firmen, lege Kaufpreis und Finanzierung fest, berücksichtige Synergien, kombiniere die Gewinne und passe sie um Zinsen, neue Aktien und Abschreibungen an. Dann vergleiche ich das Pro-forma-EPS mit dem EPS des Käufers ohne Deal.“

Kontrollfrage: Welche drei Dinge verändern durch die Finanzierung den Gewinn oder die Aktienzahl des Käufers?

Cash → entgangene Zinserträge (Gewinn sinkt). Schulden → neue Zinsaufwendungen (Gewinn sinkt). Aktien → mehr Aktien im Nenner (EPS sinkt). Alles nach Steuern.

The shortcut: compare yields

The target's earnings yield at the offer price is 67.5 ÷ 900 = 7.5% (the inverse of the 13.3× P/E paid). The deal is accretive when that yield beats the after-tax cost of whatever the buyer pays with.

A real model also combines the balance sheets: add the target's assets and liabilities at fair value, remove its equity, add goodwill, the new debt and the new shares. Then check what the deal does to leverage (net debt/EBITDA) and the credit rating.

What a buyer looks at besides EPS

  • Value: synergies minus premium M5.
  • Return: does return on invested capital exceed WACC within about three years?
  • Balance sheet: leverage after the deal, rating, covenant headroom.
  • Ownership: how much of the combined company goes to the target's holders.
  • Break-even synergies: how much must go right for zero dilution.

Interview examples

  • “Buyer P/E 10×, seller P/E 8×, all stock?” Accretive: 10 is above 8.
  • “Now the buyer pays a 30% premium.” Paid P/E is 8 × 1.3 = 10.4×, above 10, so slightly dilutive.
  • “All debt at 8%, tax 25%, target bought at 20× earnings?” Cost 6% vs. yield 5%: dilutive.
  • “What must be true for a stock deal to be accretive?” Buyer's P/E above the P/E paid, or synergies large enough to close the gap.

Purchase price allocation · Kaufpreisallokation

Purchase price for equity 900 − Book value of target equity −222.5 − Write-up of assets to fair value (brands, tech) −100 + Deferred tax liability on write-up (25%) +25 = Goodwill 602.5

Old goodwill of the target is wiped out, its equity is eliminated, written-up intangibles are amortised (which lowers reported EPS but not cash), and goodwill sits on the balance sheet until an impairment M1.

Other outputs to know

  • Exchange ratio Umtauschverhältnis: €20 ÷ €45 = 0.444 buyer shares per target share.
  • Ownership: 20m new ÷ 120m total = 16.7% for target holders.
  • Contribution analysis: target brings 18% of combined net income for 16.7% of shares.
  • Break-even synergies: the synergies needed for zero dilution.

Share deal or asset deal

  • Share deal: buy the legal entity with all liabilities. Simple transfer; seller usually prefers it for tax.
  • Asset deal: pick assets and liabilities. Buyer gets a tax step-up and leaves risks behind; contracts and staff must be transferred one by one (in Germany, employees move under § 613a BGB).

Trap. Accretive does not mean good. A high-P/E buyer can raise its EPS by buying any low-P/E company, even while overpaying. Value comes from synergies exceeding the premium M5. Say this unprompted.

How to pay: cash, stock and contingent payments

The currency decides who carries the risk between signing and closing, and afterwards. Cash leaves all risk with the buyer; stock shares it with the seller; contingent structures price the disagreement between them.

Cash BarangebotStock Tauschangebot
RiskBuyer bears all of it; seller gets a certain amount.Target holders share in overpayment and in synergies.
SignalBuyer is confident; its shares are not overvalued.Buyer may think its shares are rich. Bidder price usually falls.
ControlNo dilution of existing owners.New shareholders; may need a buyer vote above 20% new shares (US).
Tax (seller)Taxed now.Often deferred until the new shares are sold.
SpeedFast; the only route for hostile bids and buyouts.Slower: registration, valuation of the paper.

Fixed exchange ratio: number of shares fixed, value floats with the buyer's price (the norm). Floating ratio: value fixed, number of shares set just before closing. The market reads a floating ratio as confidence.

Collar: protection when closing is far away

Collar

Adjusts the number of shares for the buyer's price before closing. Fixed-value type is common; fixed-exchange type (ratio fixed inside the band, value capped outside) is rare.

CVR · contingent value right

A separate right paying out after closing. Price-protection CVR = a put spread on the buyer's share. Event CVR pays on a milestone: Pfizer–Metsera (2025) ended at $65.60 cash + up to $20.65 CVR.

Earnout · Earn-out

Extra payment if the acquired business hits targets. A call option on its future results. Common for private targets where the seller knows more and keeps running it. Playtika–SuperPlay: $700m plus up to $1.25bn. Often ends in court, since the buyer controls the numbers.

Fees and the probability of closing

Termination fees

  • Break fee (target pays buyer if it takes a better offer): typically 3–4% of equity value. Deals with one show higher premiums and less competition.
  • Reverse break fee (buyer pays if financing or antitrust fails): median 4–5%, no upper convention. A large one signals confidence.
  • Netflix–Warner Bros. Discovery (Dec 2025): $2.8bn (3.9%) one way, $5.8bn (8.1%) the other. Paramount later won at $81bn and Netflix collected the $2.8bn.
  • Fox–Roku (June 2026, $22bn): Fox pays $1,237m (5.6%), Roku pays $866m (3.9%).

What the market thinks: α

Pafter = α × Poffer + (1 − α) × Pas-is α = (Pafter − Pas-is) ÷ (Poffer − Pas-is)

Muster AG trades at €19 after the €20 offer; it was €16 before: α = 3 ÷ 4 = 75%. A merger arbitrageur buying at €19 earns 5.3% if it closes and loses 15.8% if it breaks. In a stock deal the arbitrageur also shorts the buyer.

Example: Monsanto offered $97 for Syngenta; at $92 the market implied α = 0.83. The deal was abandoned.

Deal process, due diligence and the contract

A sale process is a funnel: many buyers with little information become one buyer with full information and a signed contract. Each stage trades confidentiality against price tension.

Typical length 4 to 6 months to signing. Broad auction maximises price but leaks; limited auction balances; bilateral talks are discreet and fast but lack competition. Buy-side runs the mirror image: strategy and criteria, long list, short list, approach, indicative valuation, LOI Absichtserklärung, due diligence, financing, SPA, integration plan.

Due diligence streams · Sorgfaltsprüfung

  • Financial: quality of earnings, normalised EBITDA, net debt, normal working capital M1
  • Commercial: market, customers, competition, plan plausibility
  • Legal: contracts, change-of-control clauses, litigation, IP, licences
  • Tax: exposures, loss carry-forwards, structure
  • Plus: HR and pensions, IT and cyber, ESG, regulatory. For a gaming group, licences and compliance come first.

Findings end up in exactly three places: the price, the contract protection, or a walk-away.

The SPA · Unternehmenskaufvertrag

  • Price and mechanism (below), earnout, vendor loan Verkäuferdarlehen
  • Reps and warranties Garantien: statements the seller stands behind, with caps, baskets and time limits
  • Indemnities Freistellungen: euro-for-euro cover for known risks such as tax
  • Covenants: run the business normally until closing; non-compete
  • MAC clause: buyer may walk after a material adverse change. Rarely enforced.
  • W&I insurance: an insurer covers warranty breaches so the seller exits cleanly

Takeover law: Germany versus the US, and antitrust

Germany regulates public takeovers by statute with fixed ownership thresholds. The US leaves it to boards and Delaware judges. Name the system before you answer.

  • Minimum price: at least the 3-month volume-weighted average price before announcement, and at least the highest price the bidder paid in the prior 6 months.
  • Process: announce, file the offer document with BaFin, acceptance period of 4 to 10 weeks, reasoned statement by both boards of the target (begründete Stellungnahme), usually with a fairness opinion.
  • Defences are limited: management must not frustrate a bid (Verhinderungsverbot, § 33 WpÜG) without supervisory board approval or a shareholder vote. No US-style poison pill.
  • Market colour (Takeover Report 2026): average premium fell from 43.6% to 26.5% in 2025.

Zalando – ABOUT YOU, the full ladder in one deal. Dec 2024: voluntary offer at €6.50, a 107% premium to the €3.14 three-month average, with the stated aim of a squeeze-out. July 2025: EU clearance, closing, stake 91.45%. Nov 2025: merger squeeze-out registered, ABOUT YOU delisted.

United States: board discretion and case law

Two routes to a public target

  • Merger: negotiated with the board, proxy statement, shareholder vote. 2 to 3 months.
  • Tender offer: straight to shareholders, open at least 20 business days (Williams Act). The only hostile route. Above 90% in Delaware, a short-form merger squeezes out the rest without a vote.
  • Bear hug: a public letter to the board with a generous price (Microsoft to Yahoo, 2008, 60% premium).

Defences

  • Poison pill: if anyone crosses about 15%, all other holders may buy shares at half price, diluting the bidder. Board can adopt it overnight (Twitter, 2022).
  • Staggered board: a third elected each year, so control takes two elections.
  • Supermajority and fair-price clauses, white knight, leveraged recap, selling the prized unit.
  • Delaware statute: crossing 15% blocks a merger for 3 years unless the board agreed, the bidder got 85%, or two-thirds of other holders approve.

LBO, private equity and what deals do to debt

A leveraged buyout is a house bought with a mortgage and rented out: the tenant's cash flow repays the loan, and the owner keeps the equity that builds up.

Muster AG paper LBO. Entry at the offer: EV 967.5 = 8.06× EBITDA 120 Sources: debt 600 (5.0×) + sponsor equity 367.5 Uses: buy equity 900 + refinance net debt 67.5 Five years: EBITDA 120 → 150, debt repaid 600 → 400, exit at the same 8.06× → EV 1,209 Exit equity = 1,209 − 400 = 809 → 2.2× money, IRR ≈ 17%

Good LBO candidate

  • Stable, predictable cash flow
  • Low CapEx and working capital needs
  • Strong market position, room to improve margins
  • Clean balance sheet, assets to borrow against
  • A visible exit: sale, another fund, IPO

Fund economics: limited partners commit capital for about 10 years; the general partner earns a 2% management fee and 20% carried interest above an 8% hurdle. Strategic vs. financial buyer: the strategic can pay for synergies; the fund pays what its return target allows, which is why the LBO value is the floor in M3.

Why leverage lifts the return

Buy for 1,000, sell for 1,300 five years later, interest paid from cash flow, no repayment.

All equity: 1,000 → 1,300 = 1.3×, IRR 5%
With 600 debt: 400 → 700 = 1.75×, IRR 12%

The same 300 gain lands on a smaller cheque. It cuts both ways: sell at 800 and the all-equity owner loses 20%, the leveraged one loses half.

Using an LBO to value a company

Run it backwards. Fix the required IRR (say 20%), the leverage banks will give and the exit multiple, then solve for the highest entry price. That price is the LBO bar in the football field M3.

Exit routes: sale to a strategic buyer, sale to another fund (secondary buyout), IPO, or a dividend recapitalisation that takes cash out early by re-borrowing.

How to raise the IRR

  • Pay less at entry.
  • Use more debt, as far as cash flow can carry it.
  • Grow EBITDA faster: revenue, margins, add-on acquisitions.
  • Sell at a higher multiple.
  • Exit sooner: the same multiple of money in fewer years is a higher IRR.
  • Take dividends along the way.

IRR versus money multiple

The multiple says how much, the IRR says how fast. 2.0× in three years is 26%; 2.0× in seven years is 10%. Funds report both, because a quick flip has a high IRR but little absolute gain.

Management: keeps running the company and invests alongside the fund on favourable terms, so its payoff depends on the exit.

LBO interview examples

Paper LBO. EBITDA 100, entry at 10×, 60% debt. EBITDA grows to 150 in five years, 300 of debt is repaid, exit at 10×. Returns?

Entry EV 1,000: debt 600, equity 400. Exit EV 1,500, debt left 300, equity 1,200. Multiple 3.0×, IRR about 25%. Sources of the 800 gain: EBITDA growth 500, debt paydown 300, multiple 0.

Same deal, but the exit multiple falls to 8×.

Exit EV 1,200, equity 900, multiple 2.25×, IRR about 18%. Multiple contraction cost 300.

Why would a fund use debt at all if it has the cash?

Debt is cheaper than equity and interest is tax-deductible; a smaller equity cheque raises the return on it; the fund can spread its capital over more deals; and debt service disciplines management.

What makes a good LBO candidate?

Stable cash flows, low CapEx and working capital needs, strong market position, room for margin improvement, a clean balance sheet, a capable management team and a clear exit.

Can lenders earn more than the fund?

Yes. High-yield lenders receive 10 to 15% by contract. If EBITDA barely grows, the entry price was too high or interest eats the cash flow, the sponsor's IRR falls below that. It signals a weak or over-leveraged deal.

More leverage always means a higher IRR?

No. Beyond what cash flow can service, interest absorbs the cash that would repay debt, covenants bite, and the risk of losing the equity rises. With a low-growth company or a high entry price, extra debt can lower the return.

Bank debt versus high-yield bonds?

Bank debt is senior, secured, cheaper, floating-rate, amortising and carries maintenance covenants. High-yield is junior, fixed-rate, repaid in one amount at maturity, with incurrence covenants and usually no early repayment.

How does an LBO differ from a merger model?

The mechanics of the purchase are the same. A merger model asks what happens to the buyer's EPS; an LBO asks what return the sponsor earns on its equity at exit. There are no synergies and no combined company.

Bondholders in M&A

  • A deal can move value between shareholders and creditors with no synergy at all. A safer combined company lifts risky bonds at shareholders' expense; a debt-funded deal does the opposite.
  • On average target bonds gain (junk-rated about +4%), acquirer bonds lose, combined roughly zero.
  • Change-of-control put: bondholders may sell back at about 101% of face value if control changes. It protects them and raises the buyer's refinancing bill.
  • Ratner–Kay Jewelers (1990): the bid lifted Kay's stock 31% but offered bondholders 75 cents on the dollar; they resisted until the offer reached 90.

  4. Risk: antitrust, financing, integration.
  5. Your view: good deal or not, and what would change your mind.
M12

Fit, story and case day

More than half of an interview is about you. The technical part only decides whether your story is believed.

Three answers to have word-perfect in German

  • Lebenslauf in 90 seconds: each station in one sentence, one learning per station, end on why this role.
  • Warum M&A: from reading financial statements to asking what they mean strategically. One concrete moment, not adjectives.
  • Warum dieses Unternehmen: two of their recent deals and what you would want to learn from them.

Write full German drafts once, then rehearse them out loud instead of rewriting.

Corporate M&A versus a bank

  • In-house teams own the deal from strategy to integration; a bank's work ends at signing.
  • Fewer deals, deeper involvement, one sector.
  • Expect questions on strategy fit, integration and the industry, not only on models.
  • You are the client of banks and lawyers: judgment and coordination matter.

Behavioural answers: one structure

Situation, task, action, result, and what you would do differently. Prepare five stories and reuse them: a conflict in a team, a mistake you owned, a tight deadline, persuading someone senior, working with ambiguity. Weakness: a real one, with what you already do about it.

Drill: say the answer, then open it

Classic interview questions. Thirty seconds each, out loud.

Walk me through the three financial statements.

Income statement shows revenue to net income for a period. Balance sheet shows assets against liabilities and equity at one date. Cash flow statement starts with net income, adds back non-cash items and working capital changes, then investing and financing, and ends with the change in cash. Net income flows into retained earnings; ending cash is the balance sheet cash. M1

Depreciation rises by 10. Effects?

Pre-tax income −10, net income −7.5 at 25% tax. Cash flow: −7.5 + 10 add-back = +2.5. Balance sheet: PP&E −10, cash +2.5, so assets −7.5; retained earnings −7.5. M1

What is enterprise value and why subtract cash?

The value of the operating business to all capital providers: equity plus net debt plus other claims. Cash is non-operating and comes to the buyer, so it lowers the effective price. M2

EV is 1,000. The company raises 500 of debt. New EV?

Still 1,000 while the cash sits on the balance sheet. Debt +500 and cash +500 cancel. M2

100 shares at $10, 10 options struck at $5. Diluted equity value?

Proceeds $50 buy back 5 shares; 5 net new shares; 105 × $10 = $1,050. M2

Which valuation method gives the highest value?

Usually precedents, because they include a control premium, then DCF or comps, with LBO as a floor. But it depends on assumptions and market conditions, and a DCF can land anywhere. M3

Walk me through a DCF.

Project unlevered free cash flow for 5 to 10 years, discount at WACC, add a terminal value from Gordon growth or an exit multiple, sum to enterprise value, subtract net debt for equity value, divide by diluted shares. Then test WACC and growth. M4

How do you get to WACC, and beta for a private company?

Cost of equity from CAPM, after-tax cost of debt, weighted at target market-value structure. For a private company, unlever listed peers' betas, take the median, relever at the company's leverage. M4

Two companies are identical, but one has debt. Which has the higher WACC?

Normally the one without debt, since debt is cheaper and tax-shielded. At very high leverage the answer flips because distress risk raises both costs. M4

Why would a company acquire another?

Synergies, growth faster than building, technology or talent, market entry, an undervalued or badly run target, tax assets. Weak reasons: diversification and EPS accretion alone. M5

Buyer P/E 20×, seller P/E 16×, all stock. Accretive?

Accretive, provided 16× is the P/E at the price paid. The buyer issues expensive paper to buy cheaper earnings. If the premium pushes the paid P/E above 20×, it turns dilutive. M6

Same deal, half stock and half debt at 8%, tax 25%.

After-tax cost of debt 6%; cost of stock 1/20 = 5%; blended 5.5%. Target yield 1/16 = 6.25%. Still accretive. M6

Company A: 10 shares at $25, net income $10. It buys B (net income $10) for $150 in stock.

New shares 150 ÷ 25 = 6. Combined net income 20 over 16 shares = $1.25 versus $1.00. Accretive by 25%: A trades at 25× and pays 15×. M6

All-stock or all-debt: which is cheaper for the buyer?

Usually debt: its after-tax cost is below the cost of equity. Stock is cheaper only when the buyer's P/E is very high, so its earnings yield is below the after-tax interest rate. Debt has a capacity limit and adds risk. M6

How is goodwill created?

Purchase price minus the fair value of identifiable net assets, after writing assets up and booking a deferred tax liability on the write-up. Not amortised under IFRS; tested for impairment. M6

Revenues of 100 each merge to 250. What besides synergies?

Different reporting periods or currencies, acquisitions made in between, accounting changes such as gross versus net revenue, inflation or price increases, or simply organic growth between the two dates. M6

Why pay in stock rather than cash?

No cash or debt capacity needed, risk shared with the seller, tax deferral for the seller, useful when the buyer's shares are highly valued. Cost: dilution and a negative signal. M7

Collar, CVR, earnout: the difference?

Collar adjusts the share count for the buyer's price before closing. CVR pays after closing based on the buyer's price or an event. Earnout pays based on the acquired business's own performance. M7

The target trades at 92 after a 97 offer; before it was 68. Probability of closing?

(92 − 68) ÷ (97 − 68) = 83%. M7

Describe a sell-side process.

Preparation and buyer list, teaser and NDA, information memorandum, indicative bids, data room and management meetings, binding bids with contract mark-up, signing, clearances, closing. M8

Locked box versus closing accounts?

Locked box fixes the price at signing on a past balance sheet and protects against leakage; good for sellers. Closing accounts adjust the price after closing for actual net debt and working capital; good for buyers. M8

What happens at 30% of a German listed company?

Control under the WpÜG. The holder must make a mandatory offer to all shareholders at no less than the three-month average price and the highest price it paid in the last six months. M9

How does a poison pill work?

When a bidder crosses a trigger such as 15%, every other shareholder may buy new shares at a steep discount. The bidder is diluted so heavily that it must negotiate with the board. M9

Revlon versus Unocal in one sentence each.

Unocal: a defence must be a proportionate response to a real threat. Revlon: once the company is for sale, the board must get the best price. M9

Walk me through an LBO and its return drivers.

Set price and financing, build sources and uses, project cash flow and repay debt, sell after about five years, compute equity multiple and IRR. Drivers: EBITDA growth, multiple expansion, debt paydown. M10

Why can a strategic buyer usually pay more than a fund?

It can pay for synergies and has a lower cost of capital. A fund's price is capped by its return target and available leverage. M10

How do higher interest rates affect M&A?

Costlier debt lowers buyout prices and accretion from debt deals; higher discount rates lower valuations; sellers adjust slowly, so volumes fall and structures such as earnouts and stock deals rise. M11

You are the seller and want 100m in cash. How do you justify a higher price?

Show a credible plan and the buyer's synergies, create competition, and offer structure: an earnout or vendor loan on top of the cash, or warranties that reduce the buyer's risk. M7, M8
